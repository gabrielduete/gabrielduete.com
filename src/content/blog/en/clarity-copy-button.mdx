Hey folks, how's it going?

Lately I've been pushing my work toward the _product engineer_ side. In practice
that has meant looking at user behavior data to find pain nobody ever filed a
ticket about.

This post is about a recent case of mine that worked out really well! From
reading the heatmap to the measurement two and a half months after the deploy.

# The signal

I was digging through [Microsoft Clarity](https://clarity.microsoft.com/) on an
application from work, looking at the last 90 days, with no hypothesis in mind.
Just sweeping heatmaps for odd patterns.

And there was a red streak in a place that made no sense at all: on top of a
long numeric identifier, inside a block of user information. Static text. No
link, no button, etc... nothing clickable.

It had **2,198 clicks**! That's 0.13% of every click on the page.

![Clarity heatmap showing clicks concentrated on the identifier field](/assets/images/blog/copy-button/clarity-heatmap-1.jpg)

An element that does nothing being one of the most clicked spots on the screen
is a very loud signal. Clarity classifies part of it as a
[_rage click_](https://clarity.microsoft.com/blog/rage-clicks-user-behavior/),
several clicks in a row on the same spot, which usually means frustration.

Worth noting this didn't come from a ticket, a meeting or a user request. It
came from watching user behavior data.

# Reading the data

Quantitative data tells us **where**. It just doesn't tell us **why**. But
sometimes you can figure it out pretty fast.

My reading: the user is trying to **select and copy** that number to take it
into another system. They click, drag, miss the selection, click again, grab
half of it, try once more. Every successful copy costs a bunch of clicks.

What made me confident in that reading:

- The pattern showed up in other sections of the page too, just far less
  frequently. What was different about this section was that the content is a
  long number the user needs to take somewhere else.
- A long number is exactly the kind of content that's annoying to select with a
  mouse, especially when it sits right next to other elements.
- There was no other possible action on that element. A click there can only be
  selection.

Closing the hypothesis: there's no copy action, so the user copies by hand. And
copying by hand is expensive.

# The proposal

The solution is about as simple as it gets: a copy button next to the number.
Trades N selection clicks for 1.

I brought it to the team with two options drawn up:

**Option A: icon only**, with a "Copy number" tooltip on hover. Cleaner, doesn't
break the alignment of the information block.

**Option B: icon + label**, with the label switching to "Copied!" after the
click. More explicit, but visually heavy in an area that's already dense with
information.

The team approved it that same afternoon and picked the icon. Two good
suggestions came out of the discussion and made it into the implementation:

- keep a visual "copied" feedback even in the icon-only version, because without
  feedback the user doesn't know whether it worked and clicks again (meaning:
  you'd fix one rage click and create another);
- make that feedback disappear on its own after a few seconds, returning to the
  normal state.

I opened the PR the same day. Deploy a few days later.

# The first measurement

Two weeks after the deploy, I compared two 6-day windows, one before and one
after the button existed:

| Window                    | Button | Manual selection | Total    |
| ------------------------- | ------ | ---------------- | -------- |
| 6 days **without** button | —      | 1,928            | 1,928    |
| 6 days **with** button    | 284    | 406              | 690      |
| Change                    | —      | −79%             | **−64%** |

A 64% drop in total clicks to copy the identifier! \o/

At first glance it looks like 59% of copies are still manual, which wouldn't be
a great result.

But at that point the habit of using the button hadn't settled in yet. Six days
is too short to know.

# Two and a half months later

I went back to Clarity on October 2nd to collect data again, this time with
30-day windows. All three windows have nearly identical traffic volume, between
44k and 46k pageviews, so the raw numbers are comparable with no normalization.

| 30-day window         | Pageviews | Button  | Manual | % on button |
| --------------------- | --------- | ------- | ------ | ----------- |
| 1st month with button | 45,852    | 762     | 810    | 48%         |
| 2nd month             | 44,818    | 419     | 302    | 58%         |
| Last 30 days          | 44,073    | **855** | 69     | **93%**     |

![Panel comparing button clicks and manual selection across the three 30-day windows](/assets/images/blog/copy-button/clarity-panel-30d.png)

What this data says:

**Manual selection dropped 91%!!** From 810 to 69 clicks per month. That's about
2.3 manual clicks per day across the entire user base, so it basically became
residue! \o/

**The button became the default path.** The share of clicks going to it climbed
48% → 58% → 93%, a consistent curve month over month. That means adoption kept
rising.

**Usage grew in absolute volume.** 855 clicks in the last 30 days against 762 in
the first month, with slightly less traffic. Part of that is the button now
exists on two screens of the product, and in the first month it existed on only
one.

That's exactly the hypothesis I had raised at the first measurement: manual
copying would stay high for a while and fall as people got used to the button.
That's what happened, I just couldn't claim it until two and a half months
later.

# Method and caveats

**How the numbers were collected.** Clarity click heatmap, desktop view,
counting clicks per element: the button icon's class on one side, the text field
row on the other. When the same element appears on more than one screen, the
occurrences were summed.

**Clarity caps each heatmap query at 30 days.** That's why I compared 30-day
windows instead of one single range since launch.

**Two of the windows overlap by 12 days.** The 2nd month and the last-30-days
window share that stretch. The trend doesn't hinge on it, but it's fair to
record.

**The 1,928 clicks from the first measurement aren't reproducible today.** The
heatmap sample and the element's identity have both changed since July. So the
month-over-month comparison only uses windows measured now, with the same
method, which means the July number stands as historical context, not as a basis
for calculation.

**A click is not a copy.** One button click equals one copy; several manual
selection clicks may equal a single one. That means the real improvement in
_effort per copy_ is larger than the raw click drop suggests, and also that I
can't translate any of these numbers into "number of users".

# What we can learn from this

**Sweeping heatmaps with no hypothesis is worth it.** This pain never showed up
in a ticket, a survey or a meeting. Nobody opens a ticket saying "I had trouble
selecting a number with my mouse" haha, they just get annoyed for three seconds
and move on. Their behavior is the only place it gets recorded.

**A click on a non-clickable element is always a question.** It's the easiest
pattern to spot on a heatmap and it almost always means the user expected an
action that isn't there.

**Decide how you'll measure before you ship.** Without that 6-day window from
before the deploy, afterwards I'd have had a nice-looking number like "284
button clicks" with no basis for comparison.

**And go back and measure again.** This part really matters and sometimes we
forget. In the rush you end up shipping a lot and nobody goes back to measure
the metrics of what already shipped, the impact, etc... If I had stopped on July
22nd, I'd have recorded "−64%, but 59% still copying by hand" and been left
guessing. The two-and-a-half-month measurement is what showed the behavior
actually changed, and not just on the first novelty impulse.

And one technical detail that gave me a scare: the button's icon comes from Font
Awesome and it **doesn't render in Clarity's session recordings**, the area
looks visually empty in the replay. Clarity detects the element and counts
clicks normally, but if you go validate by watching recordings alone, you'll
think people are clicking on nothing.

# Conclusion

The deliverable itself is a copy button. A few lines of code. The UX gain is
small and localized.

What changed for me wasn't the size of the delivery, it was where it came from:
it didn't arrive ready-made in a ticket, it came from looking at data that was
already sitting there. Finding the problem was the work, solving it was much
easier. And going back to check two months later is what turned it from a hunch
into a result.

If you have Clarity, Hotjar or anything similar running on your application,
it's worth opening the heatmap when you have some time, without looking for
anything specific. Good chance you'll find something that turns into a new
feature or smooths out the user experience.
